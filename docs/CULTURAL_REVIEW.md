# Cultural review pack

This file collects every line of story, lore and Māori language in Tāne's Ascent so a reviewer can read it in one place, without playing the game.

**Who should review it:** someone who holds these kōrero, such as a kaumātua, an iwi education contact or a Māori cultural advisor. Pay them for their time.

**What to ask them:**

1. Is it appropriate to retell this story as an action game at all? If yes, under what conditions?
2. Is anything here tapu, or not ours to share in this form (for example Io-Matua-Kore, the whare wānanga, karakia)?
3. Which iwi version should the game follow, or should it keep its note that tellings differ?
4. Are the names, macrons and spellings correct?
5. Is Whiro shown fairly? Is fighting him as a boss acceptable?
6. Is the depiction of Tāne (feathered cloak, taiaha, pounamu) acceptable?
7. The music and sounds imitate taonga pūoro (kōauau, pūtōrino, pūrerehua, pahū, pūkāea) with synthesisers. Is that acceptable, or should it use real recordings, or none?
8. How should the game credit and acknowledge the source of the story?

Mark each line below with keep, change (with the new wording) or remove. The developer will apply the changes.

## Lore carvings (the lore book)

| # | Title | Text | Reviewer notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Te Kore and Te Pō | Before anything stood, there was Te Kore, the nothingness, and then Te Pō, the long night. Many whakapapa recite these ages of darkness before the world of light. | |
| 2 | Ranginui and Papatūānuku | The sky father and the earth mother lay clasped together. Their children were born and grew in the cramped dark between their bodies, with no room to stand. | |
| 3 | The children of Rangi and Papa | Among the children were Tāne of the forests and birds, Tangaroa of the sea, Tūmatauenga of people and war, Rongo of cultivated food, Haumia-tiketike of wild food, Tāwhirimātea of the winds, and Whiro. | |
| 4 | Tāwhirimātea | Not every child wanted the parents parted. Tāwhirimātea went with his father into the sky, and his storms still answer the separation. This heaven carries his anger. | |
| 5 | The separation | Tāne lay on his back, set his feet against his father and his shoulders against his mother, and pushed until they parted. Light entered between them: Te Ao Mārama, the world of light. | |
| 6 | Tāne-nui-a-Rangi | Tāne carries many names, each tied to a deed. As Tāne-mahuta he clothed his mother in forest. As Tāne-nui-a-Rangi he climbed to the heavens for knowledge. | |
| 7 | The many heavens | The number of heavens and their names differ from iwi to iwi. Many tellings count twelve, with Toi-o-ngā-Rangi as the highest, and Matangireia as the house within it. | |
| 8 | Io-Matua-Kore | Some traditions speak of Io-Matua-Kore, the parentless one, who dwells in the uppermost heaven and keeps the baskets. Other traditions do not include Io. Both are part of the wider story. | |
| 9 | Whiro | Whiro was the elder brother. He claimed the baskets by right of birth and resented Tāne for parting their parents. In many tellings he withdraws to the underworld and sends sickness and misfortune from there. | |
| 10 | Ngā Kete o te Wānanga | Te Kete-Tuauri holds sacred and spiritual knowledge. Te Kete-Tuatea holds the knowledge of ancestors, history, and trial. Te Kete-Aronui holds the arts and skills people need to live well and care for the earth. | |
| 11 | Ngā Whatu-kura | With the baskets came two sacred stones, Te Whatu-kura Huka-a-tai and Te Whatu-kura Rehu-tai. They added mana to the knowledge and were kept with it. | |
| 12 | The whare wānanga | The knowledge was taught in houses of learning. It was treated as tapu: learned carefully, held by those trained to carry it, and passed on with intent. | |
| 13 | Walking into the future | Kia whakatōmuri te haere whakamua. I walk backwards into the future, with my eyes fixed on my past. Knowledge from those who came before lights the way ahead. | |

## Loading tips and tutorial signs (gameplay only, for completeness)

- Hold toward a wall while falling to slide, then jump to kick off it.
- Jump into a ledge to grab it. Push up to climb.
- Strike from behind for a critical hit.
- Swing your taiaha at an incoming bolt to knock it away.
- Hold light to charge a stronger bolt. It costs more mana.
- Run, then crouch to slide under low gaps. Tunnels hide relics.
- Roll through attacks. You cannot be hurt mid-roll.
- Heavy strikes break a guard. Hold up or down and strike.
- Carved pou save your journey and restore your health.
- Greenstone relics raise your health. Feathers raise your mana.
- Every heaven hides one carving. Look high and look behind walls.
- Watch for the glow before a guardian strikes.
- Mana returns slowly, and faster as you land blows.

### Opening and ending lines added

- Ranginui grieved for Papatūānuku. His tears fell as rain, and still do.
- Whiro withdrew into the dark below, and looked back up once, alone.
- Whiro’s darkness rises one last time. The light of every heaven Tāne climbed comes to him.
- The kererū is the source of Tāne's double jump (Kererū Lift).

### Questions for the reviewer

1. Is it appropriate to show Ranginui and Papatūānuku together in a reflection (Rangi 5)?
2. Is it appropriate for Whiro to speak, and to take Tāne's light (Rangi 9)?
3. Should the figures who rise in Rangi 11 be called ancestors, and how should they look?
4. Is linking rain to Rangi's grief, and the kererū to Tāne, right for this telling?
5. Should the moon stones and phases (Rangi 6) relate to the maramataka, or stay generic?
